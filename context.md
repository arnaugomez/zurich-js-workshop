## 2. Institutional Context

Marta works at **Escola d’Educació Especial Can Vila**, a municipal special education school in Mollet del Vallès. The school serves children and young people with intellectual disabilities and associated conditions, and its educational purpose is not only academic instruction but also the development of personal, social, educational, and autonomy-related capacities. Can Vila serves students from ages **3 to 21**, and organizes students across areas such as integral attention and education, primary, secondary, transition to adult life, and basic interaction-with-environment skills.

The school’s educational model is highly individualized. For Marta, “progress” does not mainly mean test performance or grades in standard subjects. It often means that a student has improved in communication, autonomy, participation, self-regulation, peer interaction, motor skills, attention, emotional expression, or confidence in a real activity.

Can Vila’s own public activity updates show the kind of applied, environment-based learning that matters in this context: students participate in recurring sports and community activities, such as going to Decathlon Mollet to practice football, padel, basketball, and other activities with borrowed materials. This aligns with the teacher’s description of small-group activities and frequent outdoor or community-based experiences.

The Catalan education system also uses individualized support planning for students who need adapted educational responses. The Department of Education’s guidance for **plans de suport individualitzat** is aimed at teachers and educational professionals and frames these plans as tools for pedagogical reflection and decision-making in an inclusive education system.

## 3. Marta’s Students

Marta’s students have diverse profiles. Some have intellectual disabilities, communication difficulties, neurodevelopmental conditions, sensory needs, motor difficulties, emotional-regulation challenges, or clinical/psychological assessments that shape their educational plans.

A typical class or activity group may include students who:

- Communicate verbally, with limited speech, through gestures, pictograms, AAC tools, or behavioral cues.
- Need individualized support to participate in group routines.
- Show progress slowly and unevenly across the year.
- May not demonstrate skills through conventional academic tests.
- Need repeated practice in real situations before a skill becomes stable.
- Require careful interpretation of behavior, context, environment, fatigue, emotional state, and support level.

For Marta, a student report is therefore not just a performance summary. It is a professional narrative about development, supports, evidence, and next steps.

## 4. A Typical Week

Marta’s week is fragmented and physically active. She moves between classroom routines, outdoor activities, small-group interventions, coordination with colleagues, communication with families, preparation of adapted materials, and documentation.

A typical week includes:

**Small-group educational activities**

Activities are usually carried out in small groups because students need close observation and differentiated support. Marta adapts instructions, pacing, materials, and expectations in real time.

**Environment-based learning**

Some learning takes place outside the classroom: gardens, nearby countryside, sports facilities, community visits, collaborations with regular primary and secondary schools, or everyday autonomy contexts.

**Observation and evidence gathering**

Instead of relying primarily on exams, Marta observes what students do during activities: how they initiate tasks, follow routines, ask for help, tolerate frustration, interact with peers, complete steps, use language, regulate behavior, or transfer skills from one context to another.

**Brief note-taking**

A few times per week, usually after activities or at the end of the day, Marta writes notes. These notes are not polished. They may be shorthand, partial, inconsistent, and mixed across students and activities.

Example notes might look like:

> “Marc participated more in the garden activity today. Needed visual cue for first two steps but then watered independently. Avoided group discussion at first, later answered yes/no questions.”
> 

> “Laia had difficulty waiting during the sports activity. Better when paired with Júlia. Managed transition back to school without incident.”
> 

> “Arnau used the pictogram board to request a break twice. First time prompted, second time spontaneous.”
> 

These notes are meaningful but hard to reuse directly at report-card time.

## 5. Annual Reporting Workflow

At the end of the year, Marta prepares individualized report cards. This is one of the heaviest paperwork periods of the school year.

Unlike mainstream academic reports, these reports are:

- More descriptive than numerical.
- More individualized than subject-based.
- Focused on development, autonomy, participation, communication, and social-emotional progress.
- Based on direct observation, activity outcomes, support needs, and professional judgment.
- Written for families, other educators, and sometimes specialists involved in the student’s support network.
- Sensitive in tone: honest, respectful, specific, and constructive.

Marta’s report-writing process usually involves:

1. Opening the school’s required report template or document.
2. Looking through scattered notes from the year.
3. Reconstructing what happened in relevant activities.
4. Identifying recurring patterns, progress, challenges, and support strategies.
5. Writing a coherent narrative in professional but human language.
6. Making sure the report reflects the student accurately and respectfully.
7. Reviewing wording carefully to avoid sounding too clinical, too generic, too negative, or too optimistic.
8. Coordinating with other professionals if needed.
9. Producing the final version in the required school format.

The hardest part is not only writing. It is **turning a year of dispersed, messy, partial observations into a faithful final narrative**.

She currently uses Gemini for:

- Rephrasing rough notes.
- Organizing large amounts of information.
- Generating pedagogical activity ideas.
- Researching strategies related to psychology, pedagogy, inclusion, communication, behavior, autonomy, and student support.
- Turning fragmented notes into more readable prose.

Her experience is useful but frustrating.

## 7. Experience With AI

Marta is not anti-AI. She is already using it because it solves a real problem: report writing is time-consuming, repetitive, emotionally demanding, and cognitively heavy.

She recognizes that AI helps with:

- Getting past the blank page.
- Structuring a report.
- Summarizing large volumes of notes.
- Finding clearer wording.
- Making text more polished.
- Reducing repetitive administrative work.
- Exploring pedagogical strategies.

But she also sees serious problems.

### 7.1 Copy-paste fatigue

Gemini writes in a chatbot. Marta writes reports in Google Docs or the school’s required document format.

This creates a constant loop:

1. Copy notes from one place.
2. Paste into Gemini.
3. Prompt Gemini.
4. Copy Gemini’s answer.
5. Paste into the report.
6. Edit formatting.
7. Re-check that nothing false was introduced.
8. Repeat for each student and section.

This is especially painful when she redacts student names or sensitive details before using AI. Redaction increases effort and makes the prompt less useful.

### 7.2 Chatbot output is not naturally editable

The chatbot produces a block of text. Marta cannot comfortably review it in the same place where the final report lives.

She wants to edit, accept, reject, compare, undo, and revise in context. A chatbot answer feels detached from the actual document.

### 7.3 The generated voice feels unauthentic

AI-generated text may sound too polished, generic, formal, or emotionally flat. Marta cares about the report sounding like a teacher who knows the student, not like a corporate assistant.

She may reject phrases that are technically acceptable but feel wrong, such as:

- “The student demonstrated significant progress in socio-emotional competencies.”
- “The learner exhibits emerging autonomous behaviors.”
- “It is recommended that the student continue developing adaptive strategies.”

She would prefer text that sounds closer to her professional voice:

- “This year, Laia has become more confident when joining group routines, especially when she knows what will happen next.”
- “Marc still needs adult support to start some tasks, but once he understands the first step, he is increasingly able to continue independently.”

### 7.4 Hallucination and grounding problems

Marta’s strongest objection is invented information. She can tolerate clumsy wording. She cannot tolerate false claims about a student.

If AI invents that a student participated in an activity, improved in a skill, used a strategy, or showed a behavior that was never observed, the report becomes professionally unsafe.

For Marta, report-card AI must be evidence-grounded. It should make clear which notes or observations support each claim.

### 7.5 Sensitive student data

Marta works with minors and highly sensitive information. Some students have diagnoses, disability assessments, psychological evaluations, behavioral observations, or clinician involvement.

Even if the school’s Gemini configuration has data protections, Marta still experiences a practical and ethical concern: “Should I paste this student’s information into a chatbot?”

This is not just a legal concern. It is a professional identity concern. She feels responsible for protecting the dignity and privacy of students and families.

## 8. Trust Boundaries With AI

Marta’s relationship with AI is defined by control. She is comfortable with AI as an assistant, not as an autonomous co-author.

### Comfortable AI behavior

Marta is comfortable when AI:

- Writes a first draft from teacher-provided notes.
- Summarizes previous activities into report-ready language.
- Suggests improvements.
- Suggests a rewrite of a small selected section.
- Helps organize notes by skill area, activity, or student.
- Finds patterns across observations.
- Helps research activities and pedagogical strategies.
- Makes edits that appear as suggested changes.
- Allows her to accept, reject, undo, or redo changes.
- Clearly indicates uncertainty or missing evidence.
- Keeps the teacher in charge of the final text.

### Unacceptable AI behavior

Marta does not accept AI that:

- Invents facts about students.
- Writes directly into the final document without review.
- Rewrites her content without making changes visible.
- Makes permanent edits without version history.
- Changes the meaning or tone of her report without permission.
- Uses sensitive student data in unclear ways.
- Acts as if it knows the student beyond the evidence provided.
- Makes decisions that should belong to the teacher, psychologist, family, or educational team.

Her mental model is: **AI can prepare, propose, summarize, and suggest. It cannot decide, hide, overwrite, or fabricate.**

## 9. Product-Relevant Jobs to Be Done

### Job 1: Turn scattered notes into a report section

**When** I have accumulated many informal notes about a student across the year,

**I want** the system to find relevant observations and synthesize them into a coherent draft,

**so that** I can write an accurate report without manually rereading and reorganizing everything.

### Job 2: Keep the report grounded in evidence

**When** AI generates a statement about a student,

**I want** to see which notes or activities support that statement,

**so that** I can verify that the report is true.

### Job 3: Preserve my professional voice

**When** AI rewrites or drafts text,

**I want** it to sound like something I would actually write,

**so that** families receive a report that feels personal, respectful, and authentic.

### Job 4: Protect sensitive student data

**When** I use AI with student information,

**I want** to understand where the data goes, who can access it, and whether it is stored,

**so that** I can use the tool without feeling I am betraying student privacy.

### Job 5: Stay in control of edits

**When** AI suggests changes to a report,

**I want** the changes to appear as suggestions that I can accept, reject, or undo,

**so that** the final report remains my responsibility.

### Job 6: Reduce end-of-year paperwork pressure

**When** report-card season arrives,

**I want** the system to help me reuse the work I already did during the year,

**so that** reporting is not an exhausting reconstruction exercise.

## 10. Emotional Context

Report writing is not neutral administrative work for Marta.

It carries emotional weight because reports are read by families who may be anxious, protective, hopeful, tired, or sensitive to how their child is described. A poorly written sentence can sound dismissive, overly negative, clinical, or impersonal.

Marta wants reports to be:

- Accurate.
- Respectful.
- Specific.
- Human.
- Constructive.
- Useful for families and future educators.
- Honest about support needs without reducing the student to deficits.

She feels frustration when paperwork takes time away from teaching. She also feels discomfort when AI-generated text is fluent but not faithful.

Her ideal tool would reduce clerical burden while reinforcing, not replacing, her professional judgment.

## 11. Current Pain Points

### Fragmented evidence

Notes are dispersed across days, activities, students, and formats. Some are in documents, some in memory, some in shared team spaces, some in rough shorthand.

### Weak retrieval

When writing the final report, Marta often remembers that “something happened during that gardening activity” or “we worked on waiting turns in sports,” but finding the exact note takes time.

### Summarization burden

The cognitive work is not only writing sentences. It is deciding what matters, grouping observations by developmental area, and distinguishing one-off incidents from patterns.

### Generic AI prose

AI tends to produce fluent but generic education language. It may be grammatically correct but not specific enough to a student.

### Lack of groundedness

AI may produce plausible but unsupported claims.

### Privacy anxiety

Even when tools are institutionally provided, Marta is not fully sure what is safe to paste.

### Poor document integration

The AI workspace and the report-writing workspace are separate.

### Fear of losing control

Marta does not want AI to silently rewrite or overwrite the report.

## 12. What “Good” Looks Like to Marta

A good AI-assisted report-card tool would feel like a careful teaching assistant who has read Marta’s notes, respects confidentiality, and proposes text transparently.

It would:

- Work inside or very close to the actual report document.
- Use the teacher’s existing notes as the source of truth.
- Cite or link generated statements back to the original observations.
- Let Marta choose the scope: whole section, selected paragraph, single sentence, or specific skill area.
- Show proposed changes visibly.
- Preserve undo/redo and version history.
- Avoid writing directly into the final document without approval.
- Warn when evidence is insufficient.
- Ask clarifying questions instead of inventing.
- Learn Marta’s preferred tone from approved examples.
- Support Catalan and Spanish naturally, depending on the school’s reporting language.
- Handle sensitive data with clear institutional privacy guarantees.
- Help with research separately from student-specific writing, so general pedagogical advice is not mixed with private student records.

## 13. Likely Product Requirements

### Evidence-grounded generation

Generated report text should be traceable to teacher notes, activity records, or selected source material. Unsupported claims should be flagged or omitted.

### Suggestion-first editing model

AI edits should default to suggestions, not direct mutations. Direct edits should require explicit approval and remain reversible.

### Integrated document workflow

The tool should minimize copy-paste. Ideally, AI assistance happens inside the report document or in a side panel connected to the document.

### Privacy clarity

The product should explain data handling in plain language: what is sent, where it is processed, whether it is stored, who can access it, whether it trains models, and how long it is retained.

### Teacher voice preservation

The tool should support tone controls and examples of prior teacher-authored reports. It should avoid generic institutional language unless requested.

### Note organization

The tool should help classify notes by:

- Student.
- Date.
- Activity.
- Skill area.
- Type of observation.
- Support level.
- Evidence strength.
- Possible report section.

### Review workflow

The teacher should be able to inspect, approve, reject, edit, and undo AI contributions.

### Hallucination prevention

The system should avoid making claims that cannot be grounded in the provided notes. For missing information, it should say something like:

> “I don’t see evidence for progress in peer interaction in the provided notes. Do you want to add observations, or should I omit this area?”
> 

### Sensitive-data mode

The tool should support privacy-preserving workflows, such as local redaction, pseudonymization, or school-controlled processing, without forcing teachers to manually anonymize everything.

## 14. Example Scenario

It is June. Marta needs to prepare final reports for several students.

For one student, she has notes from classroom routines, sports activities, garden work, communication exercises, and collaboration activities with another school. The notes are useful but messy.

She opens the student’s report and selects the section on autonomy and participation. She asks the AI:

> “Draft this section using only the notes from this year. Focus on progress, current support needs, and examples from activities.”
> 

The tool generates a draft. Next to each sentence, Marta can inspect the supporting notes. One claim says:

> “He increasingly participates in group activities with reduced adult prompting.”
> 

Marta clicks the evidence and sees three notes from February, March, and May. She accepts the sentence but softens the wording.

Another sentence says:

> “He confidently communicates his preferences verbally.”
> 

Marta sees weak evidence and rejects it because the student mainly uses pictograms. The tool proposes a corrected version:

> “He has started to communicate some preferences using the pictogram board, especially when the options are familiar.”
> 

Marta accepts this version because it is accurate.

At the end, the report still feels like hers. The AI helped organize and draft, but Marta made the professional decisions.

## 16. Product Engineering Implications

This persona is not looking for a generic chatbot. She needs a **document-native, evidence-grounded, privacy-conscious writing assistant**.

The core product challenge is not text generation alone. It is the interaction model around text generation:

- Where does the AI operate?
- What source material is it allowed to use?
- How does it show evidence?
- How does the teacher control changes?
- How does it avoid hallucination?
- How does it preserve voice?
- How does it reduce copy-paste?
- How does it respect the sensitivity of student records?

For Marta, the difference between a useful AI tool and an unacceptable one is not simply model quality. It is whether the product behaves according to professional boundaries.

The tool should position itself as:

> “A controlled assistant that helps teachers transform their own observations into individualized reports.”
> 

Not as:

> “An AI that writes student reports automatically.”
> 

---

## 17. Key Design Principle

**The teacher is the author. The AI is the organizer, drafter, and reviewer.**

Every product decision should reinforce that boundary.
